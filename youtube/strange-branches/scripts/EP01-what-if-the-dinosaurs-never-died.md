## COLD OPEN

`[CLIP]` Jurassic Park: the T. rex roar. `[GFX]` freeze frame, record-scratch.

Dinosaurs never went extinct.

`[STOCK]` a pigeon eating a chip.

…Technically. Birds are dinosaurs, so there are about eleven thousand species of them alive right now, and one of them is eating your sandwich.

`[GAME]` Jurassic World Evolution 2: a herd of hadrosaurs at sunset.

But the *big* ones (the tyrants, the horned giants, the duck-billed herds of thousands) got wiped out sixty-six million years ago by a rock the size of a city.

`[GFX]` the asteroid swerving away from Earth, with a cartoon "whoosh".

So what if it missed? Would dinosaurs still rule the Earth? Would they get *smart*? And where on Earth, literally, would that leave us?

## THE RULES

`[GFX]` A "rulebook" card with three rules stamped on it.

Before we start, the rules of the thought experiment.

**Rule one:** the asteroid misses. Clean miss: no impact, no global winter, no fire raining from the sky.

**Rule two:** *everything else* still happens. The continents keep drifting, the climate keeps changing, and the planet does all the things it did in our timeline.

And that matters, because **rule three** is the uncomfortable one: Earth was already having a pretty bad time.

## PROBLEM 1: WERE DINOSAURS ALREADY DOOMED?

`[GFX]` Chapter card: **PROBLEM 1 — WERE THEY DOOMED ANYWAY?**
`[STOCK/ART]` lava fields; a map of India with the Deccan Traps highlighted.

At the end of the Cretaceous, a huge chunk of what's now India was erupting. These weren't single volcanoes but lava floods, called the Deccan Traps, which poured out enough molten rock to bury an area the size of a modern country, while pumping gases into the atmosphere.

So, asteroid or no asteroid, wasn't the party over?

`[GFX]` a "scientists arguing" split screen, with paper titles.

This is a genuine debate in palaeontology. One 2016 study argued dinosaur species were already being replaced more slowly than they were dying out, a slow fade over tens of millions of years. Other researchers pushed back: the fossil record is patchy, and fewer fossils doesn't necessarily mean fewer dinosaurs. A 2020 modelling study went further and found that the volcanoes alone probably *wouldn't* have done it. If anything, their warming might have helped offset some of the impact winter.

`[CLIP]` Jurassic Park: "Life, uh, finds a way."

So, for our branch, the verdict is that the Deccan Traps cause a rough patch, not an extinction. Some lineages disappear, as lineages always do. But the dinosaurs as a group shrug it off, the same way they shrugged off a few mass extinctions before.

Dinosaurs: one. Volcanoes: zero.

## PROBLEM 2: THE WORLD IS ABOUT TO CHANGE THE MENU

`[GFX]` Chapter card: **PROBLEM 2 — THE MENU CHANGES**
`[GAME]` Jurassic World Evolution 2: lush ferns and conifers. `[STOCK]` wind over a modern grassland.

Here's the thing about the next sixty-six million years: the world gets colder, drier and grassier. India rams into Asia and builds the Himalayas. Antarctica freezes over. And forests give way to something dinosaurs never had to deal with on a big scale: open grassland.

Grass is a terrible food. It's tough, it's low in nutrients, and it's full of tiny silica particles that grind your teeth down like sandpaper. In our world, the animals that cracked grass became horses, antelope, cattle and kangaroos.

So can a dinosaur eat grass?

`[ART]` a hadrosaur jaw with the dental battery highlighted, credited.

Funny you should ask. Duck-billed hadrosaurs had what's called a *dental battery*: hundreds of teeth packed together into a grinding surface, constantly replaced as they wore down. It's one of the most sophisticated chewing systems any animal has ever evolved, and arguably better than a cow's.

And here's the kicker: in 2005, scientists found microscopic grass fossils inside fossilised dinosaur dung from India.

`[GFX]` a microscope zoom with a cartoon "💩 + 🌾 = !!" card.

So dinosaurs were *already* eating grass. On our branch, the hadrosaurs don't need to reinvent anything; they just scale up.

`[AI]` a herd of long-legged, slender hadrosaur descendants sprinting across a meadow.

Meet the **Meadow Runner** (*Herbadromeus*): a hadrosaur that has traded its bulk for long legs and stamina, the dinosaur answer to the antelope. In our timeline, mammals filled this niche. In this one, the job was already taken.

## PROBLEM 3: THE MAMMAL PROBLEM

`[GFX]` Chapter card: **PROBLEM 3 — WHAT ABOUT THE MAMMALS?**
`[CLIP]` Walking with Dinosaurs: a small mammal scurrying at night.

Which brings us to our ancestors.

The usual story is that mammals spent the age of dinosaurs as tiny, terrified shrews, hiding in burrows and waiting for their big break. That's… partly true.

`[ART]` Repenomamus fossil, credited.

But mammals weren't *all* tiny. *Repenomamus*, an Early Cretaceous mammal about the size of a badger, was found with a baby dinosaur in its stomach. *Didelphodon*, from the very end of the Cretaceous, had one of the strongest bites for its size of any mammal ever. Mammals were trying, and some of them were jerks about it.

The problem is that every big niche (big grazer, big hunter, big anything) was occupied. In our world, the asteroid emptied those niches overnight, and mammals exploded into them within a few million years. On this branch, nothing gets emptied.

So would mammals ever break through?

Here's what this tells us for our branch. Mammals survive, but they stay specialists of the night, of burrows, of treetops and of the niches dinosaurs ignore. Expect weird ones: venomous, burrowing, gliding.

Meet the **Shade-Shrew**. It's cat-sized, nocturnal and venomous (real shrews and solenodons already are), and it might be the biggest mammal on the planet. You could hold it in your hand. You probably shouldn't.

## PROBLEM 4: DO THEY GET SMART?

`[GFX]` Chapter card: **PROBLEM 4 — DO THEY GET SMART?**
`[ART]` Dale Russell's 1982 "Dinosauroid" sculpture, credited.

Okay, the big one, and the reason half of you clicked.

In 1982, palaeontologist Dale Russell asked what would have happened if a small, big-brained dinosaur called *Troodon* had kept evolving. His answer was this: the Dinosauroid. It's upright, it has a big head and a humanoid face, and it is basically a green guy who'd ask to speak to your manager.

`[GFX]` a red "X" stamped over the Dinosauroid.

Most scientists today think this is wrong, and not because a smart dinosaur is impossible. It's because evolution isn't *trying* to make humans. Our body plan works for an ape that came down from the trees. A theropod is built completely differently: horizontal spine, tail as a counterweight, legs underneath. There's no reason to throw all of that out just to stand like us.

So what *would* a smart dinosaur look like?

`[STOCK]` a New Caledonian crow bending a hooked tool; a kea opening a box; a weaverbird tying a knot.

Well, we don't have to guess, because smart dinosaurs already exist. New Caledonian crows craft hooked tools. Ravens plan for the future. Parrots solve puzzles that stump small children. Weaverbirds literally tie knots. And they do all of it without hands.

So here's my bet. A smart dinosaur on our branch keeps the dinosaur body plan, with a horizontal posture and a balancing tail, but it frees up its hands for fine manipulation. It stays mostly feathered.

`[AI]` the Weaver: horizontal posture, long tail, beak-like snout, nimble clawed hands, weaving reeds into a dome.

Meet the **Weaver** (*Textorsaurus*), a troodontid descendant. It doesn't chip stone like our ancestors did. It weaves: nests turn into shelters, shelters into traps, and traps into towers.

But a big brain isn't free. It costs huge amounts of energy, and evolution only pays that cost if being smart solves a problem. So what problem would push a dinosaur to get clever?

`[GAME]` Path of Titans or The Isle: a pack of raptors coordinating.

The same thing that probably pushed us: *other members of its own species*. Living in groups, with alliances, rivalries, sharing food and tricking each other. Social complexity is one of the leading explanations for why primate brains got so big, and pack-hunting theropods were very likely social.

### BRIEF RANT

`[GFX]` the host's "rant mode" card.

*"Hey, are you sure about all this?"*

No! Nobody is. Intelligence evolving at all is a big "if". Life ran on Earth for billions of years before anything built a tool, and plenty of very successful lineages never needed to be clever. The honest answer is that a smart dinosaur is *possible* on this branch, not *guaranteed*.

But this is a thought experiment, and the experiment is more fun with Weavers in it. So.

## PROBLEM 5: WHO OWNS THE OCEAN?

`[GFX]` Chapter card: **PROBLEM 5 — THE OCEAN**
`[CLIP]` Prehistoric Planet: a mosasaur hunting.

Let's not forget the sea. At the end of the Cretaceous, the oceans belonged to the mosasaurs: giant marine lizards, some longer than a bus, related to modern monitor lizards and snakes.

`[ART]` a pregnant *Plioplatecarpus* fossil, credited.

Here's a detail most people don't know: mosasaurs gave birth to live young, at sea. Fossils have been found with babies still inside them. They had already cut their last tie to land, just as whales would much later.

So when the planet cools and polar waters explode with plankton and krill, which is what happened in our timeline, who takes the job of "giant filter feeder"?

`[STOCK]` a blue whale lunge-feeding.

In our world, it was whales. In this one, I'd bet on a mosasaur.

`[AI]` the Sieve King: a vast, whale-like reptile with comb-like baleen plates, feeding on krill under an aurora.

Meet the **Sieve King** (*Balaenosaurus*): a mosasaur that traded its teeth for comb-like plates and became the largest animal on the planet. It's the same problem with the same solution, just on a different branch of the tree.

## PROBLEM 6: THE COLD

`[GFX]` Chapter card: **PROBLEM 6 — WINTER IS COMING (FOR REAL)**
`[STOCK]` Antarctic ice sheets forming (time-lapse).

About thirty-four million years ago, Antarctica freezes over and the whole planet cools.

Now, you might think the cold finishes the dinosaurs off. "They're reptiles, they need sunshine!" But dinosaurs weren't the sluggish lizards of old movies.

`[ART]` Yutyrannus, credited. `[ART]` Alaskan polar dinosaur fossils.

Many were warm-blooded, or close to it. Lots of them had feathers, including *Yutyrannus*, a nine-metre relative of T. rex covered in fluff. And we've found dinosaurs that lived inside the Arctic Circle in Alaska, surviving months of winter darkness.

`[AI]` the Frost Stalker: a wolf-sized, densely feathered tyrannosaur descendant hunting in a snowy forest.

So on our branch, insulation stops being optional. Meet the **Frost Stalker**: a wolf-sized tyrannosaur descendant, fluffy and fast, that hunts Meadow Runners across the frozen steppe in packs.

Yes, it's adorable. No, you should not pet it.

## THE COMPLETED WORLD

`[GFX]` A branch-map graphic: the tree grows a new branch, and each creature pops in with a label.

So let's put it all together. Sixty-six million years after the asteroid missed, here's our present day:

- **Meadow Runners** grind the grasslands where horses would have been.
- **Frost Stalkers** hunt them through the snow.
- The **Sieve King** cruises the polar seas in place of whales.
- **Shade-Shrews** rule the night, still small and still waiting.
- And somewhere, perhaps, **the Weavers** are building towers out of living reeds.

`[AI]` a night scene: woven towers glowing with cultivated bioluminescent fungus; a young Weaver looking up at a meteor streaking across the sky.

## WHAT ABOUT US?

`[GFX]` Chapter card: **…WHAT ABOUT US?**
`[ART]` a *Purgatorius* reconstruction, credited.

Which leaves one last question: do humans ever show up?

This might be the strangest fact in this video: the earliest known primate relatives, like a tiny tree-climber called *Purgatorius*, appear right around the end of the Cretaceous. Our family tree was already sprouting in the same forests as T. rex.

So on this branch, our ancestors are *there*. They just never get the empty world that let them grow, spread and eventually build cities.

Maybe they hang on in the treetops as clever little climbers. Maybe they get eaten by a Frost Stalker. Either way, the chance of a branch that ends with *you*, specifically, watching this video, is vanishingly small.

## THE CLOSE

`[GFX]` A slow zoom out from the Weaver city to Earth, cross-dissolving to *our* Earth at night, with city lights in the same spot.

We usually tell the extinction of the dinosaurs as a tragedy. And it was, for them.

But every single thing you are, from your hands and your eyes to the brain hearing these words, exists because a rock that could have missed… didn't.

`[CLIP]` Jurassic Park: Dr. Malcolm, "Life finds a way."

Life finds a way. It just doesn't always find *us*.
